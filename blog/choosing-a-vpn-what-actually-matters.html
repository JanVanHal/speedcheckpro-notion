Choosing a VPN: what actually matters

VPNAugust 29, 2026·4 min read

By SpeedCheckPro team

Most VPN providers promise the same things: privacy, speed and security. The differences that matter are less visible, and you can check most of them yourself before you pay. This guide is a checklist, not a ranking.

Start with what a VPN does and does not do

A VPN encrypts traffic between your device and the VPN server, and websites see the server's IP address instead of yours. That is useful on public Wi-Fi, for hiding your browsing from your local network or internet provider, and for appearing to connect from another region.

It does not make you anonymous. You are shifting trust from your internet provider to the VPN provider, who can in principle see the same traffic metadata. A VPN also does not stop tracking through cookies or logged-in accounts, and it is not a replacement for antivirus software or good password habits. Deciding what you need it for makes the rest of the choice easier.

No-logs claims and independent audits

Almost every provider says it keeps no logs. The claim only means something if it can be checked. Look for:

  • An independent audit by a named security or accounting firm, with a report or summary you can actually read.
  • Scope and date. An audit of the privacy policy alone is weaker than one that inspected server configurations. An audit from several years ago says little about today's infrastructure.
  • Repeat audits, which suggest the practice is ongoing rather than a one-time marketing exercise.
  • Infrastructure choices such as servers that run from RAM only, so data does not persist on disk after a reboot.

Read the privacy policy itself as well. Some providers keep connection timestamps or bandwidth totals while still describing themselves as no-logs.

Protocols

The protocol is the method used to build the encrypted tunnel. The main options today:

  • WireGuard is modern, has a small codebase that is easier to audit, and is generally fast with quick reconnections. Many providers offer it directly or as the basis of their own protocol. Check how they handle the fact that WireGuard needs to associate your key with an internal IP address, since that affects what the server keeps in memory.
  • OpenVPN is mature, widely reviewed and flexible. It is often somewhat slower than WireGuard, but can run over TCP port 443, which helps on restrictive networks.
  • IKEv2/IPsec is well supported on mobile devices and handles switching between Wi-Fi and mobile data smoothly.

Avoid PPTP, which has known weaknesses and should not be used. Be cautious with proprietary protocols that have not been independently reviewed.

Jurisdiction

A provider is subject to the laws of the country where it is incorporated and where its servers run. That affects data retention requirements and how it must respond to legal requests. Jurisdiction matters, but it is not the whole story: a provider that genuinely collects nothing has nothing to hand over, wherever it is based. Look at jurisdiction together with audits, transparency reports and any public record of how the company has handled requests in the past.

Speed, measured your way

Every VPN adds some overhead. How much depends on the protocol, how far you are from the server, how busy that server is and your own connection. Published speed claims are hard to compare because nobody else shares your line. The reliable test is your own:

  1. Run a speed test with the VPN off and note download, upload and ping.
  2. Connect to a nearby server and test again.
  3. Try a different protocol, and a server further away if you need one.

Most providers offer a refund window. Use it to run these tests at the times of day you normally use the internet.

Leak protection and the kill switch

  • Kill switch: blocks traffic if the VPN connection drops, so your real IP is not exposed.
  • DNS leak protection: sends DNS queries through the tunnel rather than to your provider's resolver.
  • IPv6 handling: if the VPN only tunnels IPv4, IPv6 traffic can bypass it. Good apps either tunnel IPv6 or block it.

Check these yourself: with the VPN connected, an IP lookup should show the VPN server's address and none of your own, for both IPv4 and IPv6.

Everything else

  • Apps for every device you use, and how many can connect at once.
  • Clear ownership: who runs the company, and where.
  • Payment options and a straightforward cancellation process.
  • Be wary of free VPNs. Running servers costs money, and some free services fund themselves through advertising or data collection.
